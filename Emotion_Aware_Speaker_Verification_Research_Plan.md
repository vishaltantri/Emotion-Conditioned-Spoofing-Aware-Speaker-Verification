# Research Plan: Emotion-Conditioned Spoofing-Aware Speaker Verification Using Self-Supervised Speech Representations

**Document type:** Research execution plan for a potential conference paper  
**Status:** Proposed plan; novelty remains to be verified  
**Prepared:** 17 October 2026

---

## 1. Executive summary

This project investigates whether emotion-related speech information can improve **spoofing-aware speaker verification**, especially when the system encounters emotional speech and spoofing attacks generated by previously unseen systems.

The proposed system considers three kinds of evidence:

1. **Speaker identity:** Does the speech match the claimed speaker?
2. **Spoofing evidence:** Is the audio bona fide or an attack, such as text-to-speech (TTS), voice conversion (VC), or replay?
3. **Emotion/paralinguistic evidence:** Does emotion-related information add useful evidence, and under what conditions?

A self-supervised speech encoder such as WavLM or HuBERT may provide representations for downstream branches. The proposed research contribution should **not** be described as merely combining these three branches. Prior work already covers emotion-aware speaker verification, emotion-guided anti-spoofing, and integrated spoofing-aware speaker verification.

The research should instead test a specific hypothesis:

> **Research hypothesis:** Emotion-conditioned fusion of speaker and spoofing evidence can improve spoofing-aware speaker verification under cross-emotion and cross-generator evaluation, without increasing rejection of bona-fide speech to an unacceptable level.

This is a hypothesis to test, not an established result or a verified novelty claim.

### Expected outcome

At the end of this plan, the project should produce one of three defensible outcomes:

- **Positive:** The method offers statistically and practically meaningful gains over strong baselines, including in a carefully designed generalization setting.
- **Mixed:** Gains occur only for particular attack types or emotional conditions; the paper reports those boundaries honestly.
- **Negative but useful:** Emotion adds no reliable benefit, or harms robustness. This can still be useful research if the experiments are rigorous and the analysis explains why.

Do not promise novelty, improved security, or a conference acceptance before completing the prior-art review and experiments.

## 2. Problem statement

Automatic speaker verification decides whether a speech sample belongs to a claimed identity. In security-sensitive use, an attacker may attempt to pass verification using replayed recordings, synthetic TTS speech, or voice-converted speech. A speaker-verification score alone is not sufficient to determine whether the signal is bona fide.

Emotion complicates the problem. A genuine speaker may sound substantially different when calm, angry, sad, excited, or speaking in another style. Conversely, a modern speech generator may reproduce emotional cues. Therefore:

- Emotion must not be assumed to be a reliable identity marker.
- An emotion match must not automatically be treated as proof that audio is genuine.
- The system must not reject genuine speech simply because its emotional state differs from a reference recording.
- Emotion should be retained only if controlled experiments show that it adds useful evidence beyond speaker and spoofing baselines.

### Main research question

**RQ1:** Does emotion-conditioned fusion improve spoofing-aware speaker verification compared with conventional fusion of speaker and anti-spoofing evidence?

### Supporting research questions

- **RQ2:** Does any improvement persist when evaluation emotions are not represented in training?
- **RQ3:** Does any improvement persist when the test-time TTS or voice-conversion generator is unseen during training?
- **RQ4:** Which emotion conditions and attack types benefit, and which do not?
- **RQ5:** Does the proposed method preserve bona-fide acceptance and calibration at operationally relevant thresholds?
- **RQ6:** Does a shared self-supervised representation help compared with separate, established task-specific models?

## 4. Proposed method

### 4.1 System overview

Input speech  
→ consistent audio preprocessing  
→ self-supervised speech representation  
→ speaker-verification branch  
→ anti-spoofing branch  
→ emotion branch  
→ calibrated emotion-conditioned fusion  
→ accept/reject decision.

Treat this as an experimental architecture. It must be compared with conventional alternatives and may be revised based on the prior-art review.

### 4.2 Candidate encoders

Select one primary encoder for the main experiments:

- **WavLM:** A reasonable initial candidate because its pretrained representations support speaker and paralinguistic tasks.
- **HuBERT:** A possible alternative or secondary comparison if resources permit.

Avoid testing many encoders before establishing a reliable baseline. Use published pretrained checkpoints where their licensing and usage terms allow research use. Record checkpoint name, version, source, sample rate, layer selection, pooling, and whether the encoder is frozen or fine-tuned.

### 4.3 Speaker-verification branch

Purpose: score the compatibility between an enrolled/claimed speaker and the test utterance.

Possible approaches:

- A pretrained ECAPA-TDNN or another well-established speaker embedding model.
- An SSL-derived embedding with a documented scoring method, if this is a deliberate experimental condition.

Use a standard similarity score, such as cosine similarity or a validated probabilistic scoring approach. Establish the speaker-only baseline before adding emotion-conditioned fusion.

### 4.4 Anti-spoofing branch

Purpose: estimate whether the utterance is bona fide or spoofed.

Candidate approaches:

- AASIST as a strong established baseline.
- A pretrained or reproduced ASVspoof countermeasure model.
- A WavLM-based countermeasure, if supported by the implementation plan and compute budget.

Distinguish logical-access attacks (such as TTS and voice conversion) from physical-access attacks (such as replay). Do not claim robustness to attack categories absent from the evaluation.

### 4.5 Emotion branch

Purpose: estimate emotion-related information or an emotion representation.

Requirements:

- Choose a defined emotion taxonomy and label mapping.
- Document the emotion recognizer, dataset, and training source.
- Measure emotion-recognition reliability on the chosen evaluation data.
- Include uncertainty or low-confidence handling rather than assuming every emotion prediction is correct.
- Ensure emotion labels are not inadvertently leaking speaker identity, dataset identity, generator identity, or split membership.

Emotion should be tested as complementary evidence, not as a required match between a test utterance and a stored emotional template. The latter can reject genuine users whose emotional state changes.

### 4.6 Fusion strategy

Begin with a transparent conventional fusion baseline. For example, after calibration:

`S_base = a * S_speaker - b * S_spoof`

where larger `S_speaker` means a stronger claimed-speaker match and larger `S_spoof` means more spoof-like evidence. Define score directions and normalization precisely.

Then test an emotion-conditioned model, for example:

`S_proposed = f(S_speaker, S_spoof, z_emotion, u_emotion)`

where:

- `z_emotion` is an emotion representation or probability vector;
- `u_emotion` represents uncertainty, if the chosen emotion model provides a meaningful estimate;
- `f` is a small, trainable and regularized fusion model.

Possible candidates include a calibrated logistic-regression fusion model, a small multilayer perceptron, or a gated fusion function. Select one primary method based on the prior-art comparison. Do not try many methods and report only the best without accounting for model-selection bias.

A hard spoof threshold may be applied separately for operational security. Thresholds must be selected on development data, not tuned on the final test set.

### 4.7 What would count as a contribution?

A contribution may be justified if experiments establish that the proposed fusion:

- improves an appropriate integrated metric over strong speaker-plus-spoof baselines;
- retains the gain when generator or emotion conditions are held out;
- does not achieve gains merely by rejecting more genuine utterances;
- has statistically supported improvements across appropriate test subsets; and
- is reproducible with a clear protocol.

If these conditions are not met, report the outcome honestly and revise the research question.

## 5. Dataset and protocol plan

The exact datasets must be chosen after inspecting the papers and confirming access, licenses, labels, and compatibility. Avoid assuming that one dataset supplies every label needed.

### 5.1 Candidate resources

| Resource | Potential role | Important checks |
|---|---|---|
| ASVspoof 2019 LA | TTS/voice-conversion countermeasure development and baseline comparison | Official partitioning, attack IDs, labels, protocol |
| ASVspoof 2019 PA | Replay/physical-access evaluation | Replay conditions and compatibility with the threat model |
| ASVspoof 2021 LA/PA/DF | Additional attack and domain evaluation | Exact task, evaluation protocol, and availability |
| SASV 2022 resources | Integrated speaker-and-spoof evaluation | Official trial definitions and permitted training/evaluation use |
| EmoSpoof-TTS (2025) | Emotionally expressive synthetic speech | Availability, licensing, speakers, emotions, generators, and official splits |
| RAVDESS, CREMA-D, IEMOCAP, or another emotion corpus | Emotion-model training/evaluation where appropriate | License, speaker overlap, label scheme, recording domain, and whether synthetic attacks are present |

These resources are not interchangeable. An emotion corpus does not automatically provide spoof labels; a spoof benchmark does not automatically provide reliable emotion labels. Establish how each label is obtained and what assumptions it introduces.

### 5.2 Data split rules

1. Use the official train/development/evaluation partitions whenever required by a benchmark.
2. Do not train or tune on the final test set.
3. Prevent speaker leakage between partitions where the task requires speaker-disjoint evaluation.
4. For cross-generator tests, document which generators are used in training and which are held out.
5. For cross-emotion tests, explicitly define seen and held-out emotion conditions.
6. Avoid leakage from the same source recording appearing in both real and manipulated versions across incompatible partitions.
7. Keep model selection and threshold selection within training/development data.
8. Document all exclusions and preprocessing decisions.

### 5.3 Recommended evaluation tracks

- **Track A — In-domain baseline:** Standard official benchmark protocol.
- **Track B — Cross-generator:** Hold out one or more synthesis/voice-conversion systems from training.
- **Track C — Cross-emotion:** Hold out emotion conditions from training, where the data permits a defensible split.
- **Track D — Cross-dataset:** Evaluate on an independent dataset without tuning on its test labels.
- **Track E — Bona-fide emotional variation:** Measure whether genuine emotional variation increases false rejection.

If the datasets cannot support a valid track, omit it and state the limitation. Do not create a nominal “unseen generator” split when generator identity cannot be reliably established.

## 6. Experimental design

### 6.1 Baselines

At minimum, compare:

1. Speaker verification only.
2. Anti-spoofing only, reported for its own task rather than treated as a complete speaker-verification system.
3. Conventional speaker-plus-spoof score fusion.
4. The same fusion with emotion features added without the proposed conditioning mechanism.
5. The full proposed emotion-conditioned fusion.
6. A strong published integrated SASV baseline, if reproducible under the same protocol.

Where feasible, include a published emotion-aware verification or emotion-guided anti-spoofing method. If exact reproduction is not possible, clearly distinguish a reproduced baseline from a paper-reported result; do not compare numbers across incompatible datasets as if they were a controlled experiment.

### 6.2 Ablation studies

Run these controlled comparisons:

- Remove the emotion branch.
- Replace emotion-conditioned fusion with fixed fusion.
- Replace the proposed encoder with a justified alternative, if feasible.
- Remove emotion uncertainty information, if used.
- Compare frozen and fine-tuned representations only if the data and compute support it.
- Evaluate each attack family separately.
- Evaluate each available emotion group separately.
- Compare in-domain and held-out generator results.

### 6.3 Metrics

Use the official benchmark metrics wherever required. Likely metrics include:

- **EER:** Equal Error Rate; report separately for relevant component tasks and integrated SASV where applicable.
- **min t-DCF:** Tandem Detection Cost Function for the specified ASV-plus-countermeasure scenario.
- **SASV-EER or a-DCF:** Use the metric appropriate to the chosen integrated protocol.
- **FAR / FRR:** False Acceptance Rate and False Rejection Rate at a threshold selected on development data.
- **Precision, recall, and F1:** Useful supplementary measures for bona-fide/spoof classification, but not substitutes for the main verification metrics.
- **Calibration:** Use a justified calibration metric if the system outputs operational scores or probabilities.

Report the exact definition and protocol of every metric. Lower EER/min t-DCF is generally better, but metrics should only be compared under the same protocol and cost assumptions.

### 6.4 Statistical reliability

- Repeat training with multiple random seeds when feasible.
- Report mean and variation, or confidence intervals using an appropriate resampling unit.
- Use paired comparisons where trials are matched.
- Avoid treating correlated utterances from the same speaker as independent samples without justification.
- Report absolute differences and relative changes, not only the best score.
- Record compute, training time, model size, and inference latency if efficiency is relevant.

### 6.5 Success criteria

Define criteria before evaluating the final test set. A reasonable decision framework is:

- The full system must beat the conventional speaker-plus-spoof fusion baseline on the primary integrated metric.
- Gains must persist on at least one pre-specified generalization track central to the research question.
- Bona-fide false rejection must not worsen beyond a pre-defined acceptable margin.
- The ablation must show that emotion-conditioned fusion adds value beyond merely adding more parameters or an extra branch.
- Differences must be statistically and practically meaningful.

Choose the primary metric, margin, and generalization track after checking the benchmark protocol and available data. Do not invent a numerical success threshold after seeing the test results.

## 10. Risks and mitigation

| Risk | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| The closest papers already contain the proposed combination | Insufficient novelty | Complete the novelty gate before implementing the full system |
| Emotion cues are unreliable or generator-dependent | No generalization gain | Evaluate held-out emotions and generators; include uncertainty |
| Emotion branch learns speaker or dataset shortcuts | Inflated evaluation | Speaker-disjoint and dataset-aware splits; leakage audits |
| Fusion improves spoof rejection but rejects genuine emotional speech | Poor operational utility | Report bona-fide FRR by emotion and evaluate calibrated thresholds |
| Baselines are weak or inconsistently evaluated | Unconvincing comparison | Reproduce strong published baselines under matched protocols |
| Datasets lack aligned speaker, spoof, and emotion labels | Invalid or underspecified task | Use a documented label strategy and separate datasets only where methodologically defensible |
| Results vary by seed or split | Weak evidence | Repeated runs, confidence intervals, and transparent reporting |
| Compute or time is limited | Incomplete evaluation | Prioritize strong baselines, one primary fusion method, and one rigorous generalization test |

## 11. Ethics and security considerations

- Treat this as research on biometric authentication; avoid collecting voice data without informed consent and an appropriate legal basis.
- Respect dataset licenses and restrictions on biometric or personal data.
- Do not describe emotion as a reliable indicator of identity, honesty, or authenticity.
- Consider false rejection across emotional conditions, accents, languages, age groups, and recording environments when data permits.
- Define the attack threat model clearly; laboratory performance does not establish security against all real-world attacks.
- Report failure modes and limitations.
- Avoid publishing sensitive voice samples or identifying information without authorization.
